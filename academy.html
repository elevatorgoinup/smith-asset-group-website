Start here: what wholesaling actually is

Track 01 · Beginner

The one-sentence version

Wholesaling real estate means putting a property under contract with a motivated seller at a price that leaves room for a buyer, then either assigning that contract to a cash buyer or double-closing it — you're never on the title as the end owner, and you never need to fund the purchase yourself.

You are not an agent (no license needed to wholesale a contract you hold an equitable interest in) and you are not a flipper (you're not renovating or holding the property). You're the connector who makes the deal exist in the first place.

Glossary · know these before your first call
Assignment Contract

The document that transfers your equitable right to purchase a property to your cash buyer, for an assignment fee.

EMD

Earnest Money Deposit — a small good-faith deposit that shows the seller you're serious about the contract.

Buyer's List

Your roster of vetted cash buyers, sorted by what they buy (area, price, condition) so you know who to call first.

ARV

After Repair Value — what the property will be worth once it's fixed up. The anchor number for every offer.

MAO

Maximum Allowable Offer — the highest you can offer a seller and still leave your buyer enough margin to say yes.

Double Close

Two closings back to back — you actually purchase, then immediately resell — used when a deal needs more privacy on the assignment fee.

How a deal moves · illustrated

1. Find the property

Parcel research (GIS), driving for dollars, and direct outreach surface sellers before a property ever hits the MLS.

→ feeds Intermediate track

2. Put it under contract

A fair cash offer at or under MAO, a seller-friendly close date, and a signed assignable purchase agreement.

→ this is the wholesaling moment

3. Assign it to close

Your buyer's list gets first look. They sign the assignment, pay the fee, and close — you were never on title.

Sourcing systems: GIS + DealMachine

Track 02 · Intermediate

The real workflow

This is the actual pipeline Smith Asset Group LLC runs to build a target list in Richmond County, merging public parcel data with a sourcing platform:

  • Pull parcel data from Richmond County GIS / qPublic. The county's own assessor system (qpublic.schneidercorp.com) lets you search and export ownership, tax, and parcel records — free, public, and updated regularly.
  • Filter for signals, not just addresses. Absentee owners, tax-delinquent accounts, free-and-clear (no mortgage) properties, and LLC-held portfolios are the highest-signal starting points.
  • Import into DealMachine. DealMachine's List Builder pulls county-record-based lists with the same filters (tax delinquent, absentee, free-and-clear) and layers on driving-for-dollars for windshield-visible distress.
  • Skip trace before you dial. DealMachine's built-in skip tracing turns an owner name and address into a phone number and email — this is the step that makes a list actually callable.
  • Stack your lists. Cross-reference GIS signals against DealMachine's filters so the same property showing up in two sources moves to the top of your call list.

Sources: Richmond County GIS / qPublic, DealMachine Help Center.

How you talk to sellers once you've got a lead

Lead credibility beats urgency. Once a name and number come out of skip tracing, the opener should sound like a neighbor, not a script: confirm you have the right person, name the property plainly, and frame the call around convenience and speed rather than pressure — most owners worth calling today are equity-rich, not desperate, so "fast and easy" outperforms "act now."

Automating the stack & scaling out

Track 03 · Advanced

Why Zapier matters here

Your sourcing tools (GIS, DealMachine), your CRM (ReSimpli, Podio, InvestorFuse, REISift — whichever you run), and your communication tools (email, SMS, dialer) almost never talk to each other natively. Zapier is the layer that connects them: a "trigger" in one platform (a new lead, a new buyer, a status change) fires an "action" in another (create a CRM record, send a text, log a note) without you touching either platform by hand.

  • Common trigger → action chains: new website lead → create CRM record → send intro SMS → notify your phone.
  • Webhooks let a form (like the Sell/Buy forms on this site) hand data straight to a Zap without a native integration existing yet.
  • Multi-step Zaps can branch: a seller lead vs. a buyer lead routes to different CRM pipelines automatically.
  • Know the platform's real limits. Some CRM integrations are write-only (they'll accept new records from Zapier but won't let Zapier read data back out) — plan your automation around what each platform actually allows, not what you assume it allows.

Where the different platforms actually fit

Every platform in this space does one job well — the mistake is expecting one tool to do all of them:

  • DealMachine — sourcing: driving for dollars, county-record list building, skip tracing.
  • Crexi — commercial and land listings with map-based search, if you expand past single-family.
  • InvestorBase / InvestorLift-style platforms — disposition: marketing a signed contract to a large network of vetted cash buyers instead of cold-calling your own list.
  • Zillow / Redfin — public comps and market data for pricing an offer, not a sourcing or disposition tool for off-market wholesale deals.
  • ReSimpli (or your CRM of choice) — the system of record that everything above should ultimately feed into.

Scaling past one market

The systems above don't change when you expand — only the inputs do. The same GIS-plus-sourcing-platform approach works in any county that publishes public parcel and tax records, which is nearly all of them. What actually has to scale is your buyer network and your team: a virtual assistant handling skip-trace list hygiene, a dedicated acquisitions caller, and a dispositions person managing buyer relationships are usually the first three hires, in that order, before adding a second market.
